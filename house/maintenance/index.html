The ordinary work

What Every House Needs

A practical yearly rhythm for the systems and surfaces that quietly keep a home dry, safe, efficient, and functioning.

Spring

  • Inspect roof, flashing, gutters, downspouts, and exterior drainage after winter.
  • Look for foundation movement, new cracks, moisture, or grading problems.
  • Check exterior caulk, paint, siding, windows, doors, and screens.
  • Service cooling equipment and replace or clean filters as needed.
  • Test smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms and inspect fire extinguishers.
  • Check plumbing fixtures, supply lines, hose bibs, and visible drains for leaks.

Summer

  • Handle exterior painting, sealing, carpentry, masonry, and weatherproofing while conditions are favorable.
  • Inspect decks, porches, railings, steps, walks, and exterior wood for movement or decay.
  • Keep vegetation and irrigation away from siding, foundation, and woodwork.
  • Clean dryer vents and inspect appliance hoses and connections.
  • Check attic or upper spaces for heat, moisture, pests, and roof leaks.
  • Review larger repair projects while there is still time to complete them before winter.

Fall

  • Clean gutters and confirm downspouts carry water well away from the house.
  • Service heating equipment and replace filters before sustained cold weather.
  • Weather-strip doors and windows and seal obvious air or water entry points.
  • Drain or protect exterior plumbing and prepare hoses, irrigation, and outdoor equipment for freezing temperatures.
  • Inspect chimney, flue, roof penetrations, and exterior vents as applicable.
  • Test alarms again and review emergency shutoffs, flashlights, and winter supplies.

Winter

  • Watch for ice dams, roof loading, frozen plumbing, condensation, and unusual drafts.
  • Keep exterior vents and combustion-air openings clear of snow.
  • Monitor basement and crawl-space moisture and look for active leaks during thaws.
  • Listen for changes in heating, plumbing, and electrical systems rather than waiting for complete failure.
  • Keep indoor humidity reasonable to protect windows, finishes, and building materials.
  • Use the quieter season to document repairs, plan projects, and review the maintenance log.

The extra century

301 Ruby Old-House Watch List

Older houses often give warnings before they give failures. The useful habit is learning which changes are normal, which are new, and which deserve attention.

Foundation & Masonry

  • Photograph and date notable cracks so movement can be compared over time.
  • Watch mortar, brick, stone, and concrete for spalling, open joints, or moisture damage.
  • Prioritize drainage before cosmetic crack repair.

Wood, Windows & Porch

  • Check sills, trim, porch posts, rails, steps, and other horizontal wood surfaces for softness or trapped water.
  • Maintain glazing, paint, putty, weatherstripping, and hardware on older windows before replacement becomes the default answer.
  • Document original components before repair or alteration.

Plaster, Floors & Movement

  • Record new plaster cracks, door changes, floor movement, or recurring seasonal gaps.
  • Distinguish long-settled quirks from changes that are actually new.
  • Avoid forcing modern materials into assemblies that need to dry and move differently.

Water, Plumbing & Electrical

  • Know which systems are original, replaced, mixed-age, or still unidentified.
  • Record pipe, wiring, panel, fixture, and shutoff changes as they are discovered.
  • Treat unexplained staining, heat, odors, repeated breaker trips, slow leaks, and corrosion as information worth investigating promptly.

Historic Materials

  • Identify materials before sanding, cutting, demolition, or aggressive cleaning.
  • Preserve useful samples, labels, hardware, profiles, and photographs when original material must be removed.
  • Choose repair before replacement when the original material can reasonably continue to serve.

Documentation

  • Record what was found, not only what was fixed.
  • Keep costs, materials, contractor names, paint colors, product details, and photographs with each repair.
  • Give every important repair a next-check date so the house develops a usable long-term record.
