---
title: Quaking Aspen
common_name: Aspen, Trembling Aspen
scientific_name: Populus tremuloides
plant_type: tree
found_location: Root & Brass garden
habitat: |-
  - **Garden setting:** A deciduous tree for full sun with moderate, consistent moisture.
  - **Growth:** Spreads by root suckers and may form a genetically connected grove.
  - **Climate:** Native across broad areas of North America and particularly associated with cool forests, mountain slopes, and moist openings.
identification: |-
  - **Leaves:** Nearly round, finely toothed leaves on flattened petioles that flutter or “quake” in light wind.
  - **Bark:** Smooth and pale greenish-white to cream on younger trunks, becoming darker and furrowed near the base with age.
  - **Flowers:** Male and female catkins appear on separate trees before leaf-out.
  - **Harvest warning:** Never cut bark from a living trunk. Ring-barking can permanently injure or kill the tree.
benefits: |-
  - **Ethnobotanical record:** Indigenous North American traditions document quaking-aspen bark and other parts for varied pain, fever, respiratory, digestive, and topical uses.
  - **Salicylate relationship:** Aspen bark contains phenolic glycosides related to the salicylate chemistry found across the willow family.
  - **Evidence limit:** Composition varies and there is no reliable home dose. Traditional use is not equivalent to the known dose, purity, or safety of a regulated medicine.
medicinal_uses: |-
  This page records quaking aspen as a historically medicinal tree rather than recommending a home remedy. Bark preparations have been used traditionally for pain and fever, and buds or roots have appeared in topical and respiratory practices.

  Because bark chemistry is variable, tree harvest is damaging, and salicylate-type effects carry meaningful risks, internal preparation is not provided here.
parts_used: Historically inner bark, buds, and roots; observation only for this garden tree
harvest: |-
  - Do not peel or girdle the trunk or major roots.
  - Photograph and record the tree rather than harvesting it for home medicine.
  - Fallen twigs are not automatically safe or medicinally equivalent to authenticated bark preparations.
preparation: |-
  No household preparation is recommended. If a salicylate-like medicine is needed, use a regulated product under appropriate medical guidance rather than estimating a dose from garden bark.
cautions: |-
  - Avoid medicinal aspen use with aspirin or salicylate allergy, bleeding disorders, stomach ulcers, kidney disease, anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, or before surgery.
  - Do not give salicylate-containing bark remedies to children or teenagers with a viral illness because of the risk associated with salicylates.
  - Avoid during pregnancy and breastfeeding unless a qualified clinician specifically approves it.
  - Fever, significant pain, breathing symptoms, or persistent illness requires appropriate diagnosis rather than tree-bark self-treatment.
