---
title: "Marigold"
common_name: "Garden Marigold"
scientific_name: "Tagetes spp."
plant_type: annual
found_location: "Root & Brass garden"
habitat: |-
  A warm-season annual grown in full sun and well-drained garden soil. Marigolds flower from early summer until frost and are valued around vegetables and flowers for pollinator activity and companion planting. Species and cultivar affect both garden performance and safe household use.
identification: |-
  A compact to upright annual with strongly aromatic, divided, fernlike leaves and rounded yellow, orange, red, or bicolored flower heads. Tagetes foliage is distinctly pinnate and scented when rubbed. Do not confuse this plant with Calendula officinalis, also called pot marigold; calendula has simple leaves and a separate Apothecary record.
benefits: |-
  Tagetes flowers contain carotenoid pigments, including lutein in some species, along with flavonoids and other compounds studied for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. Most medicinal findings come from laboratory, animal, or concentrated-extract research rather than strong human clinical trials, so they do not establish a home treatment for illness.
medicinal_uses: |-
  Different Tagetes species have long histories of use for digestive complaints, inflammation, skin conditions, and ceremonial care. These traditions are recorded for ethnobotanical context only. Because this garden record identifies only the genus, Root & Brass does not recommend an internal remedy, wound preparation, tincture, or essential-oil treatment from this plant.
culinary_uses: |-
  Some correctly identified Tagetes species and cultivars are used as edible flowers, flavoring, tea, or natural food color. Do not eat this garden plant until its species and cultivar are confirmed as food-suitable and its growing history is known to be free of ornamental-only pesticides. Even then, use clean petals only in small food amounts and remove the bitter white base if desired.
parts_used: |-
  Flower heads or petals after species-level identification. Leaves, roots, concentrated extracts, and essential oils are not included for home medicinal use.
harvest: |-
  For a confirmed food-suitable plant, pick newly opened, undamaged flower heads after dew has dried. Choose only flowers grown without systemic pesticides or other products not labeled for edible crops. Keep diseased, moldy, insect-damaged, or roadside flowers out of preparations.
preparation: |-
  For observation, craft, or natural dye work, use fresh flower heads or dry them in a single layer out of direct sun with good airflow. For culinary use, first confirm the species and cultivar, then rinse the petals gently and use a small amount promptly. Do not distill, concentrate, or apply homemade Tagetes essential oil or extract to the skin.
cautions: |-
  Tagetes is not interchangeable with Calendula officinalis. Sap and essential oil can irritate skin or cause allergic contact dermatitis, especially in people sensitive to ragweed, chrysanthemums, daisies, or other Asteraceae plants. Some Tagetes extracts and essential oils can also be phototoxic; do not apply them before sun exposure or use them in homemade sunscreens. Avoid internal medicinal doses during pregnancy, breastfeeding, childhood, or alongside medication because safety and dosing are not established. Stop use for rash or irritation and seek urgent care for trouble breathing, facial or throat swelling, faintness, or a rapidly spreading reaction. This record is educational and is not medical advice.
