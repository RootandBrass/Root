Harvest Guide

Mint

Harvest: Spring through early fall

Gather: Young leaves and tender stems before flowering for the strongest flavor.

Uses: Tea, culinary use, drying, infused preparations, and household blends.

Calendula

Harvest: During active bloom

Gather: Fresh flower heads on a dry day after morning dew has evaporated.

Uses: Drying, crafts, seasonal preparations, decorative blends, and personal apothecary records.

Marigold

Harvest: During active bloom

Gather: Fresh flower heads on a dry day after morning dew has evaporated.

Uses: Drying, crafts, seasonal preparations, decorative blends, and personal apothecary records.

Honeysuckle

Harvest: While flowers are fresh and open

Gather: Clean blossoms before they begin to fade.

Uses: Fragrant preparations, drying, garden crafts, and recorded traditional uses.

Raspberry

Harvest: When berries release easily

Gather: Fully colored ripe berries.

Uses: Fresh eating, preserves, baking, freezing, syrups, and seasonal kitchen projects.

Strawberry

Harvest: When fully red and ripe

Gather: Fruit during the cool part of the day when possible.

Uses: Fresh eating, preserves, baking, freezing, and seasonal recipes.

Blackberry

Harvest: When fully dark and easily picked

Gather: Ripe fruit without crushing the berries.

Uses: Fresh eating, jam, sauces, baking, freezing, and preserves.

Frost Peach

Harvest: When fruit is firm but not hard and color is darkening

Gather: Fully colored ripe peaches.

Uses: Fresh eating, preserves, baking, freezing, syrups, and seasonal kitchen projects.

PRESERVATION WORKBENCH

Preserving the Harvest

Choose the method for the plant and the way it will eventually be used. Start with correctly identified, clean, unsprayed material and label every batch before it leaves the worktable.

DryingFreezingInfusingGarden Reference

01

LOW MOISTURE · LONGER KEEPING

Drying

Best for aromatic herbs, sturdy leaves, and flowers that will be used in teas, blends, crafts, or carefully prepared topical products.

Air Dry

  1. Harvest after the dew has dried, preferably before the day becomes hot.
  2. Remove damaged material. Rinse only when needed, then dry the surface completely.
  3. Tie four or five small stems together or spread flowers in a single layer.
  4. Keep them in a dark, dry, well-ventilated place away from cooking steam and direct sun.

Use a Dehydrator

  1. Arrange plant material in one layer without crowding.
  2. For herbs, begin around 95–115°F; humid conditions may require slightly more heat.
  3. Check frequently rather than relying on one fixed drying time.
  4. Cool completely before packing so warmth does not create condensation.

Ready when: Leaves crumble, stems snap, petals feel papery, and no cool or soft center remains.

Store: Whole when possible in airtight, labeled containers away from heat, moisture, and light.

Check: Look again after 24 hours. Condensation, softness, clumping, or a musty smell means the batch needs more drying or should be discarded.

02

FRESH COLOR · QUICK STORAGE

Freezing

Best for ripe berries, peaches, and herbs whose flavor matters more than their thawed texture.

Berries & Peach Slices

  1. Use sound, ripe fruit. Wash gently, drain well, and remove stems, hulls, pits, or bruised pieces.
  2. For loose fruit, freeze in a single layer on a tray before transferring to freezer containers.
  3. Treat peach slices with an ascorbic-acid product according to its directions if browning matters.
  4. Pack in freezer-grade containers, remove excess air, label, and freeze promptly at 0°F or below.

Herbs

  1. Rinse only if needed and dry thoroughly.
  2. Freeze whole leaves or sprigs in small labeled portions, or chop them into an ice-cube tray.
  3. Cover herb cubes with water for later use in soups, sauces, teas, or cold drinks.
  4. Expect thawed herbs to be soft; use them for flavor rather than garnish.

Best system: Freeze the amount most likely to be used at one time so the rest stays sealed.

Label: Plant, variety, preparation, amount, and freeze date.

Use first: Any package with heavy frost, excess air, or signs that it partially thawed and refroze.

03

FLAVOR · FRAGRANCE · EXTRACTION

Infusing

An infusion moves a plant's flavor, aroma, color, or soluble compounds into water, vinegar, syrup, or oil. The storage rule depends on the liquid.

Water & Tea

Use only positively identified edible plants. Make small batches with clean equipment, refrigerate leftovers promptly, and discard anything cloudy, fizzy, slimy, or off-smelling.

Vinegar

Use commercial vinegar of known acidity and clean plant material. Unless following a tested processing recipe, treat homemade flavored vinegar as refrigerated—not shelf-stable.

Syrup

Garden syrups are perishable unless made and canned with a tested recipe. Refrigerate a fresh batch or freeze it in practical portions.

Oil

Fresh herbs, flowers, or garlic in oil can support botulism growth. Never store a fresh homemade infusion at room temperature. Use a tested acidification method, or refrigerate immediately and use within four days; freeze for longer storage.

Important: “Infused” does not automatically mean edible, medicinal, or shelf-stable. Keep topical preparations clearly marked external use only, and never copy the storage rules for one liquid onto another.

Before Anything Goes Into a Jar

  • Confirm the exact species and the correct plant part; a common name is not enough.
  • Use only clean, healthy material that has not been treated with unsafe garden chemicals.
  • Never guess at canning time, acidity, or pressure. Use a current tested recipe.
  • When a batch shows moisture, mold, gas, leaking, bulging, or an unexpected odor, do not taste it.
