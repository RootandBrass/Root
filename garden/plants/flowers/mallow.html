--- title: Mallow scientific_name: Malva spp. plant_group: flowers plant_type: Flowering herbaceous plant bloom: Summer into fall light: Full sun to partial shade water: Moderate; established plants can tolerate some dryness show_apothecary: true common_name: Mallow habitat: |+ Mallows in the genus Malva commonly grow in gardens, fields, disturbed ground, roadsides, and open sunny places. Most tolerate ordinary garden soil and many handle both dry and moderately moist conditions. identification: |+ - **Leaves:** Usually rounded to kidney-shaped or shallowly lobed, with visible palmate veins and long leaf stalks. - **Flowers:** Five-petaled flowers, commonly pink, lavender, purple, or pale in color, often with darker veins. - **Fruit:** Distinctive flattened, round seed clusters often compared to a tiny wheel or cheese wheel. - **Growth:** Depending on species, plants may be low and spreading or more upright. - **Identification note:** This entry is kept at *Malva* genus level until the exact species growing at 301 Ruby is confirmed. Apothecary and edible use should be based on a positive species identification. benefits: |+ - Malva species are rich in mucilage, the slippery plant compounds responsible for their traditional soothing and demulcent use. - Traditionally valued for soothing irritated tissues of the throat, respiratory tract, digestive tract, and urinary tract. - Leaves and flowers have a long history of external use for irritated or inflamed skin. - Several common Malva species are also edible and provide mild, mucilaginous greens. medicinal_uses: |+ **Traditional use:** - Leaves and flowers of several *Malva* species have traditionally been used as demulcents to coat and soothe irritated mucous membranes. - Traditional preparations have been used for dry or irritated throats and coughs and for irritation of the digestive or urinary tract. - Leaves have also been used externally as soothing poultices for minor skin irritation, inflammation, bruises, and insect bites. - These are traditional herbal uses and should not be treated as proven treatment for disease. Uses vary by species. culinary_uses: |+ - The young leaves of several *Malva* species are eaten raw or cooked and generally have a mild flavor. - Their mucilage thickens soups and stews somewhat like okra. - Young immature seed heads of some species are edible and have a mild, nutty flavor. - Flowers of appropriately identified edible *Malva* species may be used as a garnish. - Confirm the exact species before eating plants from the garden. magical_uses: |+ **Folklore & magical associations:** - Traditionally associated in folk magic with love, gentleness, protection, healing, and peaceful domestic energy. - Its soft leaves and soothing mucilage make it a natural symbolic herb for comfort, reconciliation, and emotional softening. - May be incorporated symbolically into protection, healing, home-blessing, and self-care workings. - These associations belong to folklore and modern magical practice rather than medical evidence. parts_used: Leaves and flowers primarily; young shoots and immature seeds are edible in some Malva species harvest: |+ - Harvest young, healthy leaves before they become tough or damaged. - Flowers may be gathered when freshly opened. - Gather only from plants positively identified and growing in clean soil away from herbicides, roadside runoff, and animal waste. - Leave enough flowers and seed heads for pollinators and reseeding. preparation: |+ - **Food:** Young leaves may be washed thoroughly and used fresh or cooked after species identification. - **Traditional infusion:** Dried or fresh leaves and flowers have historically been infused in water to extract their soothing mucilage. - **Cold-water preparations:** Mucilaginous herbs are often prepared in cool water because the mucilage readily disperses into water. - **Topical traditional use:** Clean leaves have historically been crushed or softened for external poultices. - Leaves and flowers can be dried gently and stored dry, dark, and airtight for later reference or traditional herbal use. cautions: |+ - **Confirm the species before internal use.** “Mallow” describes many plants, and this record currently represents the genus *Malva* rather than one confirmed species. - Traditional use does not establish effectiveness for treating a medical condition. - Because mucilage can potentially alter how quickly oral medicines are absorbed, separate concentrated mallow preparations from medications as a precaution. - Avoid harvesting from contaminated or chemically treated ground. - Some Malva species growing in heavily nitrogen-enriched soil may accumulate higher nitrate levels in their leaves. - Stop use if an allergic or unusual reaction occurs. sources: |+ -
