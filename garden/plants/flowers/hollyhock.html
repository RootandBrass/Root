Traditional soothing preparations generally relied on water-based infusions or decoctions to extract the plant's mucilage. This record does not provide medicinal dosing. Do not use moldy, rust-damaged, or chemically treated plant material. show_apothecary: true common_name: "Common Hollyhock, Garden Hollyhock" habitat: |- Hollyhock prefers full sun, good air circulation, and fertile, well-drained soil. It often behaves as a biennial or short-lived perennial but readily reseeds where conditions suit it. Tall flowering stalks may need staking in windy locations. Water at the soil line rather than over the leaves because crowded, damp foliage encourages hollyhock rust. identification: |- Hollyhock forms a basal clump of broad, rounded to heart-shaped leaves with shallow lobes and a softly rough texture. Mature plants send up tall upright flower stalks, often 4 to 8 feet or more, lined with large single or double flowers in colors ranging from white and yellow to pink, red, purple, and nearly black. Flowers have the classic mallow-family form with five petals in single-flowered types and a prominent central staminal column. Round, flattened seed pods develop after bloom. Confirm the plant as *Alcea rosea* rather than assuming every mallow-family ornamental has the same traditional uses. benefits: |- Hollyhock contains mucilaginous compounds, especially in the flowers, leaves, and roots. In traditional herbal practice, mucilage-rich plants were valued for coating and soothing irritated tissues. Hollyhock has therefore been recorded historically for dry throat, cough, digestive irritation, and external skin comfort. These uses are based mainly on traditional practice and related mallow-family chemistry rather than strong modern clinical trials specifically on *Alcea rosea*. medicinal_uses: |- Traditional herbal records describe hollyhock flowers, leaves, and roots as soothing demulcent materials. Water-based preparations were historically used for dry or irritated throat, cough, and digestive discomfort, while cooled preparations were sometimes applied externally to irritated skin. Modern evidence specifically supporting hollyhock as a treatment is limited, so these should be treated as historical and traditional uses rather than established medical therapy. culinary_uses: |- Clean, unsprayed petals are sometimes used as an edible garnish, and young leaves have historical food use in some traditions. Only use plant material that is positively identified and has not been treated with ornamental pesticides or systemic chemicals. Culinary use is separate from medicinal-dose use. magical_uses: |- Hollyhock is commonly associated in modern folk and garden magic with protection of the home, abundance, resilience, ambition, growth, and the strength to stand tall through difficult conditions. Because it often grows beside houses, fences, and gates, it is also used symbolically for household guardianship and boundary protection. Its tall flowering stalks make it a natural emblem of aspiration and persistence. These are folkloric and spiritual associations, not medical effects. cautions: |- Correct identification matters because the mallow family includes many related species and traditional uses are not automatically interchangeable. Use only unsprayed garden material; ornamental plants may have been treated with pesticides not intended for food or medicinal use. Hollyhock rust is extremely common, and diseased leaves should not be harvested. Mucilage-rich herbs may theoretically slow absorption of oral medicines, so separate medicinal preparations from prescription drugs unless a qualified healthcare professional advises otherwise. Safety of medicinal-dose use during pregnancy, breastfeeding, and childhood is not well established. Seek appropriate medical care for persistent cough, trouble breathing, significant throat swelling, severe abdominal pain, bleeding, fever, or worsening symptoms. personal_notes: |- Hollyhocks have been part of the Root & Brass garden since about 2019.
