Root harvest should be limited to mature plants intentionally grown for that purpose. - Leave plenty of flower heads for pollinators, seed production, and winter birds. preparation: |- - **Record first:** Confirm the echinacea species before choosing a plant part or preparation. - **Drying:** Dry clean leaves, petals, and flower heads in a single layer away from direct sun. - **Product distinction:** Do not copy doses from a commercial extract onto homemade tea or tincture; their concentrations are not comparable. show_apothecary: true common_name: Echinacea, Purple Coneflower habitat: |- - **Garden setting:** A hardy herbaceous perennial for full sun to light shade. - **Soil:** Prefers well-drained soil and becomes drought-tolerant after establishment. - **Season:** Flowers from midsummer into autumn and provides seed for birds when heads remain standing. identification: |- - **Flower head:** A raised, firm, bristly central cone surrounded by drooping or spreading ray petals. - **Leaves:** Usually rough-textured and lance- to oval-shaped, with visible veins. - **Stems:** Upright and often slightly hairy. - **Species note:** Medicinal research most often involves *E. purpurea*, *E. angustifolia*, or *E. pallida*. Confirm the exact species before applying species-specific preparation advice. benefits: |- - **Respiratory research:** Echinacea products may slightly reduce the chance of catching a cold, but it remains unclear whether they shorten a cold once it begins. - **Traditional use:** Several echinacea species have a long record of use for upper-respiratory symptoms and topical wound care. - **Evidence limit:** Results depend on species, plant part, extraction method, and product quality. A garden infusion is not equivalent to a standardized extract used in research. medicinal_uses: |- Echinacea is most commonly recorded for short-term use around ordinary cold symptoms. Modern evidence is mixed and does not support using it to treat influenza, pneumonia, strep throat, or another serious infection. Traditional topical use is also documented, but homemade preparations should be limited to minor skin care and never placed into deep or infected wounds. cautions: |- - Echinacea can cause digestive upset and allergic reactions, occasionally severe. Risk may be higher for people allergic to ragweed or other Asteraceae plants. - Ask a clinician before use with autoimmune disease, an immune-system disorder, organ transplantation, or medicines that suppress or alter immune function. - Consult a pediatric clinician before giving echinacea to a child. Rashes and potentially serious allergic reactions have been reported. - Pregnancy and breastfeeding safety data are limited. Do not use it as a substitute for vaccination, testing, antiviral treatment, antibiotics, or urgent respiratory care. sources: |- 1.
