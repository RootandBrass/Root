--- title: Calendula scientific_name: Calendula officinalis photo: /images/uploads/calendula-1787337510640.png plant_group: flowers plant_type: Cool-season annual bloom: Late spring until frost light: Full sun to partial shade water: Moderate; water when the top inch of soil is dry garden_notes: Calendula is a cheerful, cool-season annual with orange to golden-yellow daisy-like flowers. It grows best in mild weather and often continues blooming until a hard frost. Deadheading encourages repeat bloom, and plants may self-sow when some seed heads are allowed to mature. seasonal_record: Watch for first bloom, peak flowering, heat-related slowdown, fall rebound, first frost, and volunteer seedlings the following season. show_harvest: true harvest_season: Throughout active bloom, from late spring until frost parts_used: Fresh flower heads and petals harvest: Harvest fully opened flower heads on a dry morning after the dew has evaporated. Choose clean, healthy, unsprayed flowers. Pinch or snip the heads just below the flower base. Frequent harvesting also encourages the plant to continue producing blooms. harvest_uses: Drying, infused oils, salves, teas, culinary garnish, crafts, ritual work, and seasonal preparations preserve_dry: Dry petals or whole flower heads in a single layer with good airflow, away from direct sunlight. A dehydrator may be used on a low herb setting. Store only when completely crisp and dry, in a labeled airtight container away from heat, moisture, and light. preserve_freeze: Freezing is possible for decorative or culinary use, but calendula generally keeps its quality better when dried. Fresh petals may be frozen into ice cubes for drinks or decorative use. preserve_infuse: Fully dried flowers are commonly infused into oil for topical preparations. Keep all plant material below the oil surface and use completely dry flowers to reduce spoilage risk. Calendula may also be infused into tea, vinegar, honey, or culinary preparations as appropriate. preparation: For dried flowers, separate clean petals or dry whole flower heads until fully crisp. For tea, use dried petals or flower heads in hot water. For topical preparations, dried calendula is commonly infused into a carrier oil and then used as-is or incorporated into salves and balms. Label every preparation with the plant, part used, date, and intended use. show_apothecary: true common_name: Pot Marigold, Garden Calendula habitat: Calendula is native to the Mediterranean region but is now widely cultivated in temperate gardens. It prefers full sun, though it tolerates partial shade, and grows best in fertile, well-drained soil with moderate moisture. It favors cool to mild temperatures and may slow or stop flowering during intense summer heat before reblooming as temperatures cool. identification: Calendula officinalis is an annual in the daisy family, Asteraceae. Plants usually form branching clumps of slightly sticky, aromatic, oblong to lance-shaped green leaves. Flower heads are typically bright yellow, gold, or orange with many ray florets surrounding a central disk. The curved, irregular seeds are distinctive and often resemble small hooks or curled crescents. Calendula should not be confused with Tagetes species, the plants commonly called French or African marigolds. benefits: Calendula is valued as a versatile garden and apothecary plant. Its flowers have a long history of topical use for soothing minor skin irritation and supporting the care of dry or chapped skin. The petals are edible, attract beneficial insects and pollinators, provide natural color in foods and crafts, and dry well for year-round preparations. medicinal_uses: Calendula flowers have traditionally been used externally in washes, compresses, infused oils, creams, and salves for minor skin irritation, superficial wounds, chafing, and dry or inflamed skin. Herbal traditions also use calendula as a mild tea or gargle. Modern herbal references most consistently support its traditional topical use rather than treating it as a substitute for medical wound care. Preparations should be used only on clean, minor skin concerns and not on deep, infected, or serious wounds. culinary_uses: Unsprayed calendula petals are edible in normal food amounts. Fresh or dried petals can be scattered over salads, soups, rice, eggs, breads, cakes, and other dishes. They may also be used to add golden color to butter, cheese, tea blends, infused vinegar, or decorative salts and sugars. The flavor is mild, slightly peppery, and sometimes faintly bitter. magical_uses: In European folk and modern magical traditions, calendula is associated with the sun, warmth, joy, protection, healing, success, confidence, and divination. The golden flowers are commonly used in solar workings, protection charms, healing sachets, altar offerings, dream work, and rituals intended to bring optimism or clarity. Folklore practices vary by tradition and are best recorded as cultural or spiritual associations rather than medical effects. cautions: Calendula belongs to the Asteraceae family. People with known allergies to ragweed, chrysanthemums, daisies, or related plants may also react to calendula. Stop use if redness, itching, swelling, or other irritation develops. Use only correctly identified Calendula officinalis and avoid flowers treated with pesticides or other chemicals when preparing food or herbal products. Do not use home preparations as a replacement for professional care of deep wounds, severe burns, spreading redness, pus, fever, or other signs of infection. Internal medicinal use during pregnancy or breastfeeding should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional because safety data are limited. personal_notes: Calendula is one of the most useful crossover plants in the Root & Brass garden because the same flowers can move from the garden into the kitchen, harvest shelves, apothecary, and grimoire.
