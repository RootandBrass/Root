--- title: Yarrow scientific_name: Achillea millefolium common_name: Common Yarrow plant_group: flowers plant_type: Hardy herbaceous perennial bloom: Early summer through fall light: Full sun water: Low to moderate; drought tolerant once established garden_notes: Yarrow is a tough, aromatic perennial with finely divided, fernlike foliage and broad, flat-topped flower clusters. It handles poor soil, wind, cold, and dry conditions unusually well, making it especially useful in exposed high-altitude gardens. Plants spread by rhizomes and seed, so they may wander beyond their original planting. seasonal_record: Record spring emergence, first bloom, peak flowering, pollinator activity, fall color, seed production, and any areas where the plant spreads aggressively. show_harvest: true harvest_season: Leaves can be gathered before flowering; flowering tops are commonly harvested in early to midsummer parts_used: Leaves and flowering tops harvest: Harvest only correctly identified, unsprayed plants from clean ground. For flowering tops, cut healthy stems when most of the flower cluster is freshly open and dry after morning dew has evaporated. Leaves may be gathered earlier in the season when young and clean. harvest_uses: Drying, tea blends, infused oils, salves, compresses, crafts, floral arrangements, and ritual work preserve_dry: Tie small bundles or spread leaves and flower heads in a single layer with good airflow away from direct sunlight. Store only when fully dry and brittle in a labeled airtight container. preserve_freeze: Freezing is generally less useful than drying for yarrow, though fresh leaves can be frozen for non-medicinal craft or reference use. preserve_infuse: Fully dried yarrow may be infused into oil for topical preparations or steeped in hot water as a traditional herbal tea. Keep dried material fully submerged in oil to reduce spoilage risk. preparation: Dried flowering tops and leaves are commonly used in teas, washes, compresses, infused oils, and salves. Label every preparation with plant name, plant part, harvest date, and intended use. show_apothecary: true habitat: Achillea millefolium is widespread across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere and commonly grows in meadows, roadsides, open fields, disturbed ground, and dry sunny sites. It tolerates lean soil, cold winters, and drought once established. identification: Yarrow usually grows in clumps of upright stems with deeply divided, feathery leaves that have a strong herbal scent when crushed. Flower heads are arranged in dense, flat-topped clusters and are commonly white, cream, pale pink, or deeper cultivated colors. Confirm identification carefully because members of the carrot family can be dangerous lookalikes from a distance, though yarrow foliage and flower structure differ on close inspection. benefits: Yarrow is valuable as a drought-tolerant perennial, pollinator plant, cut flower, and traditional herbal species. It attracts beneficial insects, tolerates difficult growing conditions, and has a long history of use in folk preparations for minor skin concerns and digestive complaints. Modern clinical evidence is limited, so traditional uses should not be treated as proven disease treatments. medicinal_uses: Yarrow has traditionally been used externally for minor cuts, abrasions, bruises, and irritated skin, and internally as a bitter or digestive herb and in preparations for colds and fever. Evidence from modern clinical research is limited. Home use should be restricted to minor concerns, and serious wounds, infection, persistent bleeding, or significant illness require professional care. culinary_uses: Young leaves have been used in small amounts as a bitter, aromatic herb and older traditions include yarrow in beverages. The flavor is strongly bitter and resinous, so culinary use is usually modest. Only correctly identified, uncontaminated plant material should be used. magical_uses: In European folk and modern magical traditions, yarrow is associated with protection, courage, boundaries, healing, love, divination, and warding. It appears in charms, protective bundles, ritual baths, and divination practices. These are cultural and spiritual associations rather than medical effects. cautions: Yarrow belongs to the Asteraceae family, so people allergic to ragweed, chrysanthemums, daisies, marigolds, or related plants may react. Skin contact can occasionally cause irritation or increased sun sensitivity. Internal medicinal use during pregnancy is generally avoided because safety is uncertain. Yarrow may theoretically interact with anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs and other medications. Avoid using concentrated preparations without professional guidance if pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medications, or managing a medical condition. Never harvest from roadsides, sprayed areas, or contaminated ground. personal_notes: Yarrow is a strong fit for the Root & Brass garden because it combines cold hardiness, drought tolerance, pollinator value, apothecary use, and old-fashioned cottage-garden character.
