--- title: Milk Thistle scientific_name: Silybum marianum common_name: Milk Thistle, Marian Thistle, St. Mary's Thistle plant_group: weeds plant_type: Annual or biennial thistle bloom: Late spring through summer light: Full sun water: Low to moderate; drought tolerant once established show_apothecary: true habitat: |+ - Prefers sunny, disturbed ground and well-drained soils. - Common along roadsides, fields, waste areas and other disturbed sites where it has naturalized. - Native to the Mediterranean region and now naturalized in many parts of the world. - Can reseed aggressively, so seed heads should be managed where spread is unwanted. identification: |+ - **Leaves:** Large, glossy green leaves conspicuously marbled or veined with white; margins are deeply lobed and armed with sharp yellowish spines. - **Flowers:** Large solitary purple to reddish-purple flower heads surrounded by very spiny bracts. - **Growth:** Robust upright thistle that may reach several feet tall under favorable conditions. - **Fruit/seed:** Dark achenes with a pale tuft; these seed-like fruits are the principal medicinal material. - Confirm identification before harvesting. Several thistles look superficially similar, and the white-marbled foliage is one of milk thistle's most useful field marks. benefits: |+ - Milk thistle is best known for the flavonolignan mixture **silymarin**, concentrated in the mature fruits/seeds. - Silymarin and its major component silybin have antioxidant and cell-protective activity in laboratory and animal research. - Milk thistle has a very long history of traditional use for liver and biliary complaints. - Human studies of silymarin for chronic liver diseases have produced mixed or inconclusive results; traditional reputation should not be treated as proof that it treats liver disease. - Research continues into anti-inflammatory, metabolic and other possible biological effects, but many proposed benefits remain investigational. medicinal_uses: |+ **Traditional and historical use:** - Fruits/seeds have been used for centuries in European herbal traditions for liver and gallbladder complaints and as a general liver tonic. - Preparations made from the seed are the source of silymarin used in modern milk-thistle supplements. **Modern evidence note:** - Laboratory and animal research provides substantial evidence that silymarin has antioxidant and hepatoprotective mechanisms. - Human trials in chronic hepatitis C and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease have not consistently shown meaningful improvement in disease activity or progression. - This entry therefore records milk thistle as a historically important medicinal plant with active constituents, not as a proven treatment for liver disease. culinary_uses: |+ Milk thistle has historical use as a food plant. Young leaves have been eaten after the spines are carefully removed, and other tender portions have also been used traditionally. Mature plants become extremely spiny. Positive identification and careful preparation are essential; the Apothecary record focuses primarily on the mature fruits/seeds rather than routine culinary use. magical_uses: |+ **Folklore and modern magical associations:** - Protection, boundaries and resilience, drawing obvious symbolism from the plant's formidable spines. - Strength, endurance and recovery after hardship. - Purification and protective household or garden workings. - Historically associated with the Virgin Mary in European Christian folklore, reflected in names such as Marian thistle and St. Mary's thistle. These are folkloric and spiritual associations rather than medical effects. parts_used: Mature fruits/seeds are the principal medicinal part; leaves and other tender portions have historical food uses harvest: |+ - Wear sturdy gloves and protective clothing; mature leaves and flower bracts are sharply spined. - Harvest only plants positively identified as *Silybum marianum* and growing where collection is permitted. - For seed collection, allow flower heads to mature and dry until the fruits are fully developed but collect before they disperse widely. - Bagging mature seed heads before cutting can reduce accidental seed spread. - Avoid plants exposed to roadside pollution, herbicides, pesticides or contaminated soil. - Because milk thistle can spread aggressively, responsible harvesting should prevent unwanted seed dispersal. preparation: |+ Traditional and commercial medicinal preparations primarily use the mature fruits/seeds. Modern silymarin products are extracts standardized for particular flavonolignans, and their chemistry and concentration are not equivalent to a simple homemade tea. For preservation as whole herbal material, thoroughly dry mature harvested fruits/seeds and store them in a labeled airtight container away from heat, light and moisture. This record does not provide a medicinal dose or concentrated-extract recipe because product strength, health conditions and medication interactions can materially affect safe use. cautions: |+ - Milk thistle belongs to the Asteraceae family. People allergic to ragweed, daisies, chrysanthemums and related plants may be more likely to react. - Reported adverse effects are usually mild and may include nausea, diarrhea, heartburn or other gastrointestinal upset; allergic reactions can occur rarely. - Milk thistle or silymarin may interact with some medications. Anyone taking prescription medicines, especially drugs with a narrow therapeutic range, should check with a pharmacist or other qualified healthcare professional before medicinal use. - Evidence for treating chronic liver disease is mixed; milk thistle should not replace medical evaluation or treatment for liver or gallbladder disease. - Pregnancy and breastfeeding safety data are limited compared with ordinary foods; medicinal use warrants professional guidance. - The plant itself is physically hazardous because of its sharp spines. Wear gloves when harvesting. - Confirm identification before any internal use. sources: |+ - LiverTox: Clinical and Research Information on Drug-Induced Liver Injury. Milk Thistle.
