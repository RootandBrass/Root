--- title: "Milk Thistle" scientific_name: "Silybum marianum" common_name: "Milk Thistle, Marian Thistle, St. Mary's Thistle" plant_group: weeds plant_type: "Annual or biennial thistle" bloom: "Late spring through summer" light: "Full sun" water: "Low to moderate; drought tolerant once established" show_apothecary: true habitat: |- Milk thistle prefers open, sunny, disturbed ground and well-drained soil. It commonly appears along roadsides, field margins, waste ground, vacant lots, and other places where soil has been disturbed. Once established it can tolerate dry conditions well. identification: |- Milk thistle forms a large basal rosette of broad, shiny green leaves marked with distinctive white marbling along the veins. Leaf margins are deeply lobed and armed with sharp yellowish spines. Mature plants can become tall and branched, producing large purple flower heads surrounded by very spiny bracts. The white-veined leaves are one of its most recognizable features. benefits: |- The mature fruits, often called seeds, contain a mixture of flavonolignans known collectively as silymarin. Milk thistle has a long history of use for digestive complaints and for supporting liver function. Modern research has studied silymarin for liver disease, metabolic conditions, and other uses, but clinical results are mixed and there is not enough high-quality evidence to conclude that milk thistle treats liver disease or other health conditions. The plant also provides nectar and pollen for insects while in bloom. medicinal_uses: |- Traditional European herbal medicine uses milk thistle fruit for digestive discomfort, fullness, and indigestion, and historically for support of liver function after serious disease has been excluded. Modern supplements are usually standardized extracts of the fruit rather than simple garden preparations. Human clinical trials for hepatitis, fatty liver disease, alcohol-related liver disease, and other liver conditions have produced conflicting or limited results, so this plant should be recorded as a traditional and researched herb rather than a proven liver treatment. culinary_uses: |- Historical food use includes young leaves, stalks, roots, and flower bases after careful removal of the spines. Because mature plants are extremely spiny and identification matters, this garden record treats food use as historical reference rather than a routine kitchen recommendation. magical_uses: |- In European folk tradition, milk thistle is associated with protection, resilience, strength, purification, and boundaries. Its sharp spines make it a natural symbolic plant for warding and defensive work, while the white-veined leaves have inspired Marian folklore connected with protection and nourishment. These associations are spiritual and folkloric rather than medical effects. parts_used: "Mature fruits/seeds are the principal medicinal part; leaves and other tender portions have historical food uses" harvest: |- Wear sturdy gloves and long sleeves because the leaves and flower bracts are sharply spined. For seed harvest, allow flower heads to mature until the heads dry and the seed tufts begin to loosen, then cut the heads into a paper bag and finish drying them in a dry, well-ventilated place. Separate the fruits from chaff only after they are fully dry. Harvest only from unsprayed plants away from contaminated roadsides or treated ground. preparation: |- Commercial medicinal preparations commonly use dried, powdered fruit or standardized extracts containing measured amounts of silymarin. A homemade tea is not equivalent to a standardized extract because silymarin is poorly extracted in water. For this apothecary, dried fruits can be stored whole in a labeled airtight container in a cool, dark, dry place. Avoid improvising concentrated extracts or copying supplement doses into homemade preparations. cautions: |- Milk thistle may cause digestive upset such as nausea, bloating, or gas and can trigger allergic reactions, especially in people sensitive to ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, daisies, or other members of the Asteraceae family. Drug interactions are possible, so medicinal use should be discussed with a pharmacist or clinician when prescription medicines are involved. Safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding is not well established. Supplements can also vary significantly in purity and silymarin content. The plant itself is very spiny, so gloves are advisable during harvest. sources: |- 1. NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health — Milk Thistle: Usefulness and Safety. 2.
