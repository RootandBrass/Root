--- title: "Common Purslane" scientific_name: "Portulaca oleracea" common_name: "Little Hogweed, Purslane" plant_type: "Annual succulent" bloom: "Summer" light: "Full sun" water: "Low" show_apothecary: true habitat: "Common purslane is a low-growing annual succulent that thrives in warm, sunny, disturbed ground. It commonly appears in gardens, paths, cracks, cultivated beds, roadsides, and other dry or compacted soils. It tolerates heat and drought exceptionally well but can also grow vigorously in fertile garden soil." identification: "Purslane forms spreading mats with smooth, fleshy reddish or green-red stems. Leaves are thick, succulent, smooth-edged, and spoon- to wedge-shaped. Small yellow flowers usually have five petals and open in bright sunlight. Seed capsules contain many tiny dark seeds." benefits: |- - Nutritious edible green containing alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 fatty acid uncommon at relatively high levels in leafy terrestrial plants. - Provides carotenoids and vitamins and minerals, with composition varying by growing conditions and plant maturity. - Contains flavonoids, alkaloids, organic acids, terpenoids, sterols, polysaccharides, and other phytochemicals studied for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity. - Has a long history as both food and traditional medicine in Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, Persian, Chinese, Ayurvedic, and other traditions. - Traditionally valued as a cooling, soothing plant and used for digestive, urinary, inflammatory, and skin complaints. - Laboratory, animal, and limited human research has investigated possible metabolic, gastroprotective, liver-protective, kidney-protective, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects. These findings are promising but do not establish purslane as a treatment for those conditions. medicinal_uses: "Purslane has an unusually broad ethnomedicinal history. Traditional records describe the aerial parts or juice as cooling, soothing, astringent, and mildly diuretic. Historical uses include diarrhea and dysentery, digestive irritation, urinary complaints, feverish or inflammatory conditions, minor skin irritation, sores, and other complaints." culinary_uses: "Purslane is a traditional food plant with a crisp, succulent texture and pleasantly tart, slightly lemony flavor. Young leaves and tender stems may be eaten raw in salads, sandwiches, yogurt dishes, and herb mixtures or cooked in soups, stews, egg dishes, sautés, and vegetable preparations. Its natural mucilage can slightly thicken soups and stews. It is also traditionally pickled in some cuisines." magical_uses: "In later European folk-magic and modern herbal-magic traditions, purslane is commonly associated with protection, peace, sleep, happiness, love, and calming conflict. Some traditions place it near entrances or carry it as a protective herb; others associate its cooling nature with soothing anger or restoring emotional balance. These are folkloric and spiritual associations rather than medical effects." parts_used: "Young leaves, tender stems, and aerial portions; seeds also have traditional food uses" harvest: "Harvest young, tender leaves and stems from positively identified plants before they become tough or heavily seeded. Pick from clean areas that have not been treated with herbicides, pesticides, road chemicals, or contaminated runoff. Rinse thoroughly. For fresh culinary use, harvest shortly before eating. If collecting for an apothecary record, note the location and growing conditions because wild purslane readily colonizes disturbed and potentially contaminated ground." preparation: "Fresh purslane is best suited to culinary preparations and traditional fresh-plant applications. Wash it thoroughly and remove damaged or questionable material. It can be eaten raw or cooked as a vegetable." cautions: "Correct identification is essential. Do not confuse purslane with prostrate or spotted spurge (Euphorbia species), which produces a characteristic milky latex and should not be eaten." personal_notes: "Purslane is a useful example of a plant that can be both an unwanted garden weed and a valuable edible/apothecary species. Record where it volunteers, how aggressively it spreads, flavor and texture at different harvest stages, and whether harvested plants came from soil known to be free of herbicides and other contamination." sources: "Iranshahy M, et al.
