--- title: "Dandelion" scientific_name: "Taraxacum officinale" common_name: "Common Dandelion" plant_group: "weeds" plant_type: "Perennial broadleaf weed" bloom: "Spring through fall, often in repeated flushes" light: "Full sun to partial shade" water: "Adaptable; established plants tolerate dry periods" garden_notes: |- Dandelion forms a low basal rosette and a deep taproot, allowing it to return after mowing or incomplete removal. The flowers are valuable early-season forage for many insects, while the wind-dispersed seed heads make the plant extremely good at volunteering wherever it pleases. seasonal_record: |- Note first spring bloom, repeat flowering after mowing or rain, seed-head production, and locations where plants repeatedly return from established taproots. show_apothecary: true habitat: |- Common in lawns, gardens, roadsides, disturbed ground, compacted soils, and open sunny places throughout temperate regions. Dandelion tolerates a wide range of soil conditions and develops a substantial taproot. identification: |- A basal rosette of deeply toothed leaves surrounds leafless hollow flower stalks. Each stalk carries one bright yellow composite flower head and exudes white milky latex when broken. Mature seed heads form the familiar round white "puffball" of parachute-like seeds. Confirm identification before harvest because other yellow-flowered members of the daisy family can appear similar at a glance. benefits: |- Dandelion is useful as an edible wild green in ordinary food amounts, an early nectar and pollen source, and a traditional herbal plant. Leaves, flowers, and roots have all been used in food and folk preparations. Laboratory research has explored antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, but strong clinical evidence for treating health conditions is lacking. medicinal_uses: |- Dandelion leaves, flowers, and roots have a long history in traditional herbal practice. Preparations have been used for digestive complaints and as a bitter digestive herb, while the leaves have also been used traditionally in diuretic preparations. Modern clinical evidence is limited, and dandelion should be recorded as a traditional-use herb rather than a proven treatment for disease. culinary_uses: |- Young leaves can be eaten raw or cooked and become more bitter as they mature. Flowers may be used in fritters, syrups, jellies, teas, and other traditional preparations. Clean roots have historically been roasted for a bitter coffee-like beverage. Harvest only correctly identified plants from areas free of herbicides, pesticides, road runoff, pet waste, and other contamination. magical_uses: |- In folk and modern magical traditions, dandelion is commonly associated with wishes, divination, resilience, transformation, perseverance, solar energy, and communication. Seed heads are especially associated with wish-making and dispersing intentions. These are cultural and spiritual associations rather than medical effects. parts_used: "Leaves, flowers, and roots" harvest: |- Harvest young leaves before flowering when a milder flavor is wanted. Gather open flowers on a dry day after dew has evaporated. Roots are commonly dug when plants are well established, often in spring or fall. Use only unsprayed plants from clean ground and positively identify the species before using it internally. preparation: |- Leaves may be washed and used fresh or dried for later use. Flowers can be used fresh or dried in a single layer with good airflow. Roots should be scrubbed thoroughly, sliced into smaller pieces, and dried completely before storage; dried root may also be roasted for culinary use. Label stored material with plant name, part, location, and harvest date. cautions: |- Dandelion in normal food amounts is generally considered likely safe, but safety of larger medicinal amounts is less certain. It belongs to the Asteraceae family, so people sensitive to ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, daisies, or related plants may react. Dandelion may theoretically interact with medicines including diabetes drugs, anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs, and diuretics. Safety of medicinal amounts during pregnancy and breastfeeding is not well established. Avoid harvesting from chemically treated or contaminated ground, and seek professional guidance before using concentrated preparations alongside medications or for a health condition. personal_notes: |- Keep dandelion in the Weeds group so it stays out of the ornamental Plant Journal while remaining available in the Apothecary. Record where it grows, whether the area has ever been treated, and which plant parts are harvested. sources: |- National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), Dandelion: Usefulness and Safety. Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, Plants of the World Online: Taraxacum officinale. additional_notes: |- Dandelion is a classic example of a plant that is both persistent lawn weed and useful apothecary subject. The strongest reason to keep careful records is harvest safety: plants growing in sprayed lawns, roadsides, or contaminated soil should not be used for food or herbal preparations. ---
