--- title: "Prickly Lettuce" scientific_name: "Lactuca serriola" common_name: "Prickly Lettuce, Wild Lettuce" plant_group: "weeds" plant_type: "Annual or biennial broadleaf weed" bloom: "Summer into early fall" light: "Full sun to partial shade" water: "Adaptable; commonly found in disturbed and moderately dry soils" garden_notes: |- Prickly lettuce often begins as a basal rosette, then bolts into a tall branching plant. It reseeds readily and commonly appears along disturbed edges, paths, alleys, roadsides, and neglected soil. The leaves can orient vertically, and mature plants may become several feet tall. seasonal_record: |- Record the rosette stage, bolting date, flowering period, seed production, and the locations where new seedlings appear the following season. show_apothecary: true habitat: |- Frequently found in disturbed ground, vacant lots, roadsides, field edges, gardens, and waste places. It tolerates sun, heat, and relatively dry conditions once established and spreads readily by wind-carried seed. identification: |- Lactuca serriola typically forms a rosette before sending up a tall branching stem. Leaves are alternate, often deeply lobed, and have a distinctive row of prickles along the underside of the central midrib. Broken leaves or stems release white milky latex. Small pale-yellow flower heads form in branched clusters and later produce tufted wind-dispersed seeds. Correct identification matters because prickly lettuce can be confused with sowthistles and other Lactuca species. benefits: |- Prickly lettuce has a long history of traditional herbal use, particularly in preparations associated with calming, sleep, coughs, and digestive or spasmodic complaints. Laboratory and animal research has examined some of its constituents, but reliable human clinical evidence is limited. It is best documented in the Apothecary as a traditional-use plant rather than as a proven medicine. medicinal_uses: |- Traditional references describe prickly lettuce as having been used as a sedative, hypnotic, cough suppressant, expectorant, demulcent, antispasmodic, diuretic, and purgative. These uses come largely from historical and folk practice. Modern evidence in humans is insufficient to establish effectiveness or safe medicinal dosing, so concentrated or internal medicinal use should be approached cautiously. culinary_uses: |- Young leaves have sometimes been eaten as a bitter wild green, but they become increasingly bitter and tough as the plant matures and latex production increases. Because species identification and individual sensitivity matter, prickly lettuce is better treated primarily as an apothecary study plant rather than a routine edible. Do not harvest from sprayed or contaminated sites. magical_uses: |- Modern folk and magical practice sometimes associates wild lettuces with rest, dream work, quieting, boundaries, and release because of their historical connection with sleep and calming preparations. These associations are cultural or spiritual rather than evidence of a medical effect. parts_used: "Leaves, aerial parts, and historically the dried latex" harvest: |- If recording or harvesting for study, collect only positively identified, unsprayed plants from clean ground. Young leaves are easiest to handle before bolting. Historical preparations sometimes used the milky latex from mature plants, but concentrated latex should not be treated as a casual home remedy because potency and safety are poorly standardized. preparation: |- For reference collections, leaves or aerial parts may be dried in a single layer with good airflow and stored only when fully dry. Historical sources describe dried latex preparations, but home preparation of concentrated latex is not recommended because composition and strength can vary considerably. Label all retained material clearly with species, plant part, location, and date. cautions: |- Human safety data for medicinal use of Lactuca serriola are limited, and there is no well-established safe therapeutic dose. Avoid concentrated preparations, especially dried latex, without qualified guidance. Use extra caution with sedatives, sleep medicines, alcohol, or other substances that depress the central nervous system because traditional use suggests sedating effects, even though interaction data are limited. Avoid medicinal use during pregnancy or breastfeeding because safety is not established. Prickly lettuce belongs to the Asteraceae family and may cause reactions in people sensitive to related plants. Correct identification is essential, and roadside or chemically treated plants should never be used internally. personal_notes: |- Keep prickly lettuce in the Weeds group so it does not appear in the Plant Journal.
